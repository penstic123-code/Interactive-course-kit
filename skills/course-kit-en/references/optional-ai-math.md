# Optional content rules: worldview weaving and the five steps of maths

Purpose: two practices that only some courses need, moved out of content-rules.md. Read Part 1 when a course (AI, cognition, behaviour and the like) weaves in knowledge that lets readers "see the world differently"; read Part 2 when a course has formulas and readers differ in mathematical background. Skip what you don't use, and record what you chose in the project document.

## 1. Worldview knowledge base and weaving rules (formerly content-rules 1D)

This kind of knowledge lets readers "see the world differently", and the user especially likes it. It is woven into each chapter's "why it works" and **spirals back** in 2–3 situations. A principle with enough weight can become its own "worldview chapter" (a law the reader can verify by hand, not a philosophical ramble), set up earlier by letting the reader meet it in concrete phenomena (without naming later chapters) and referred back to later. Verify sources online before use.

**Weaving rules**:
1. Build a "principle–chapter" matrix: each important principle by default appears in ≥2 chapters in different situations (the spiral is what gives it weight), and each chapter carries 0–2 principles: only when the chapter's concepts really have a principle to hang on it, never to fill the matrix.
2. A principle appears under a concrete phenomenon ("Why are gacha pulls so compelling?" → prediction error).
3. On its second appearance, refer back to the first and take a new angle.
4. Cite sources and controversies; when there is a relationship map, register the principle as a node in it, linked to the concepts that use it.
5. Worldview chapters also follow the five-layer structure (content-rules section 1, item 9); when the law can be checked first-hand, add an interaction that verifies it.

## 2. Maths climbs five steps (formerly content-rules 1C)

The goal is **mathematical intuition**: what it resembles, why it has this shape, and what happens when one number changes.

1. Present the situation and the trouble first, so the reader needs a method;
2. Work the example through with concrete numbers, no letters;
3. Point out the pattern and swap words for symbols one at a time, ≤2 new symbols each time, saying on the spot how each is read and which number it stands for;
4. Turn key formulas into a "formula breakdown" interaction: formula fragments and plain-language explanations linked by matching colours;
5. Work it again with the formula and a live diagram (move a slider, the diagram changes at once).

The five steps are the full climb for a hard formula; for a simple expression the reader sees at a glance, merge steps 2 and 3 and skip steps 4 and 5. Before any mathematical concept or symbol appears, prepare the ground with at least one of a real-world situation, a visualisation or a numerical example, and give more the harder and more abstract it is. Settle the reader's mathematical starting point first, and treat anything beyond it as new knowledge to prepare on the spot. Better one formula fewer than one formula the reader can't follow.
