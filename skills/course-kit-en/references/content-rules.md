# Interactive learning web courses: content rules

Purpose: content rules for interactive learning web courses (what to teach, how deep to go, acceptance criteria for a chapter and for a whole course, and the workflow). Rules and numbers specific to one course go in that course folder's `项目规则与经验.md` (generated by `starter/`). You can copy this whole file into your project and use it as `content-rules.md`.

These rules are for building interactive learning web courses in **any field**, one folder per course.
Reader: an ordinary person who wants to learn a field systematically. Assume no background, but go to real depth, and keep it **interesting to read and easy to follow**.

- Sections 1 to 9 are general rules that don't depend on style. Section 10 covers choosing a style and shared aesthetics; the actual look comes from each course's style.
- A "chapter" below means the course's unit of learning, which can also be a level, a lesson or whatever unit the course defines. The rules come in three tiers: **principles** (required, with flexible ways of meeting them), **optional practices** (used when the content suits them) and the **technical floor** (accessibility and check-script thresholds, which do not move). The numbers, structural order and file names here are **defaults and reference values**, and each number says what it protects; change one if the course does not need it, and record the change in the project document.
- Prose style follows the `en-teaching-prose` skill (classic style + narrative non-fiction); Chinese editions use `zh-teaching-prose`. This file doesn't repeat its details.
- Visuals, components, layout, pictures, interactions, motion, skins and the one-step check are in course-kit's `SKILL.md`, and this file doesn't repeat them.
- Tools live in course-kit's `tools/`: `check.mjs` for the one-step check, `qa.mjs` for automated click tests, `shot.mjs` for full-page or single-element screenshots, `readable.py` for the reader view, and `illus-overlap.mjs` for illustration overlap checks; the browser is found by `browser.mjs` (Edge or Chrome, or set the `EDGE_PATH` environment variable). `check.mjs` runs the three checks of section 8 in one go.

## 1. Content principles (the most important part)

The first eight items are **principles**: they decide whether the reader can follow, they hold for every chapter, and how you meet them is flexible. Write them into the chapter outline before drafting (section 4) and check them in review (section 8). The last four cover completeness and accuracy.

1. **One question per chapter; each section answers a question the previous one raised.** Why: readers hang new material on a slot that is already waiting for an answer; an answer to no question can only be memorised. The question chain is a planning and review tool. In the outline, each section gets one line: "question now → answer → question left"; the question uses only words the reader has now. The prose need not open every section with an explicit question (avoid "so the question is…" as a habit); the link comes from the previous section really leaving something and the next one picking it up, so the text reads straight through with the headings covered. A chapter can also be a guided tour around one theme (a history, a case study), and then the "question" can be broad. Within the first sentence or two of a section, the reader should know what the section answers; a situation may open a section, but by default it takes a sentence or two and is not built up into a small paragraph (when the section is itself telling a story or case, the scene may run longer, but its first sentence still tells the reader why it is being told).
   ✓ "The four elements say what a game is made of, not why it is fun", followed by why games are fun.
   ✗ A section ends on a question, and the next one turns to something the reader has not yet wondered about.
   ✗ A section spends four or five sentences on a scene, and the reader learns what it is answering only in the third paragraph.
2. **Experience first, name second.** Why: a name is a label for something the reader has already grasped; given earlier, it is stored as an empty word. For each new concept: trouble → the reader meets it first-hand (a scene, a hand calculation or an interaction) → name → make it exact → edges. The interaction can be that experience (play the definition tester first, then Wittgenstein). Interaction titles, "Try it" goals, buttons and exercises do not use a **new** word the prose has not yet named; names the reader already knows ("AI", "game") may come first, since the rule is about new terms. That first-hand experience must be short: within the first screen the reader knows which concept the chapter teaches. The chapter title names it (names in titles and headings only orient the reader and do not count as naming; naming means the first highlighter-marked appearance in the text, with a concept card), the opening scene, analogy or task takes only a few sentences, and the main concept is named and given its concept card right after; the following sections then take it apart (what it is, what parts it has, why each part is needed). Before the name first appears, there are no several interactions and no long set-up; when a concept can only be grasped after the reader has calculated or played it once by hand, that one time goes before the name, equally short, and the reader knows what to look for.
   ✗ A task "get the loss below 0.5" appears before the prose says "this score is called the loss".
   ✗ Three interactions are done before the main concept is first named, and the reader is half a page in without knowing what the chapter teaches.
3. **One new thing per paragraph; numbers to remember go in a figure.** Why: working memory holds only a few items at once (about four chunks). A new term, new symbol, new operation, or a number to carry to the next sentence each counts as a "new thing". The default grain follows the reader's level: the stronger the reader's background, the more a paragraph may carry, but never two unfamiliar ideas in one paragraph that depend on each other. Numbers the reader needs later go in a table, figure or readout, and sentences only point to them; a hand calculation reveals one step at a time.
   ✗ One paragraph that computes predictions, then errors, then squares them, then explains why squares, then averages: five new things in a row.
4. **Make it solid before building on it.** Why: if the next concept uses one that still feels "just heard", the reader is building on sand. Let the reader use the depended-on concept once by hand (a hand step, a small interaction, a judgement) before teaching what depends on it; everyday experience the reader already has needs no special arrangement. If that cannot be done, split the chapter.
5. **Length follows difficulty.** A chapter's budget is estimated in difficulty points (1A), not by counting concepts. Difficulty points are a planning estimate, not a hard cap: use them to decide where to split a chapter or give a concept more room; ≤ 8 is a default warning line that can be adjusted after reader review; over the line, consider splitting first, and if not splitting, state the reason in the outline; never pad or split mechanically to hit a number. A prerequisite is either taught in an earlier chapter (one sentence back-reference, plus a reminder figure or table where readers easily forget) or taught in full here as a new concept, never squeezed into a compressed paragraph.
6. **Every element serves understanding or the reading experience.** Why: an element that helps neither takes attention, and readers must guess why it is there. Each row of the outline's element table says which question in the chain it answers, or what it gives the reading (an illustration that lets the eye rest after dense text, a recap that helps memory, a playful touch that holds interest), what the reader does or looks at, and what they understand or feel afterwards. Pure decoration that does neither is cut; the number of elements follows the content, with no quota.
7. **Mark a concept's edge with positive and negative examples.** Why: with one example the reader mistakes the example for the concept; a counterexample or near miss shows where the concept stops (homework has a goal, rules and feedback, but lacks "voluntary", so it is not a game). Every core concept of difficulty ≥ 2 gets at least one such counterexample or near miss, by default ≥ 3 concrete examples, concrete enough to picture; one example is enough for a concept of difficulty 1. One main example may run through the whole chapter and be referred back to, and each new example says something the main one cannot. Sources follow the course's level: introductory courses draw mostly on everyday life and ready-made situations in the field; academic or maths-heavy courses can use academic examples (classic worked problems, real datasets, experiments from papers, a typical exam-style problem). Which kind a course uses is its own choice, recorded in the project document.
8. **Let the reader know whether they understood.** Why: reading is not understanding, and readers cannot always tell the difference. Every core concept of difficulty ≥ 2 gets a moment where the reader uses it and gets feedback: a "Try it" task, a prediction or an exercise, not reading alone; a concept of difficulty 1 can be covered by the end-of-chapter exercises. An optional close: have readers apply the chapter's concept to something of their own ("turn an everyday chore into a game: write down its goal, rules and feedback"), the most direct test of transfer, with a "Your thoughts" box.
9. **Teach a core concept fully: all five layers** (a self-check after writing, not the order of sections and not a quota; headings carry no layer labels):
   - **What it is**: definition + intuitive example + diagram.
   - **Why it is needed**: what trouble it solved. When there is reliable history, tell the **invention story**: reconstruct it from the learner's side. What problem people faced, why earlier attempts failed, the key insight, and how it evolved into today's form, so the reader feels it **had to be invented**. Where the reader can make a guess and the guess can expose a wrong intuition, let them guess or try first, then reveal. Add an illustrated timeline or a "problem → solution → new problem" evolution diagram. The body keeps one or two sentences of the key insight, and the full story goes into an invention-story box collapsed by default. With no reliable history, explain in the body the pain it solved.
   - **Why it works**: the underlying principle (psychology, maths, systems, physics and so on, depending on the field), **with its source** (who, which book or study); add a verification experiment when the reader can confirm it by hand and gains something from doing so. Core concepts of difficulty ≥ 2 go through this layer by default; when there is truly no underlying principle with a citable source (a way of dividing things up, say), explain why it is divided this way and on what basis, and do not invent a source.
   - **How to use it**: real situations + interactions and practice; the reader does it once by hand (item 8).
   - **Edges**: limits, controversies, common misconceptions (the counterexample of item 7 counts). A controversy is discussed right next to the view it challenges, and the end-of-chapter misconceptions section holds only misunderstandings that cut across concepts. Say "this is X's view" once, where needed. When a study has a replication controversy, say so plainly.
   A concept of 1 difficulty point is done once it has "what it is + why it is needed + one example"; secondary concepts do not get all five, and the rest goes into side paths.
10. **Focused and systematic, covering the whole course**: each chapter tells its story around one theme, starting from a familiar situation and unfolding layer by layer. Draw the knowledge map of the whole course first (parts, dependencies, order). Intuition and examples come first, then the definition, then the principle. Whole-course coverage: basic concepts → underlying principles → worldview knowledge → case studies → hands-on projects. When setting the table of contents, list "what practitioners run into every day" and check it for gaps.
11. **Factual accuracy**: verify uncertain names, years, numbers and quotations online, preferring primary sources such as original papers and official documentation. If a fact can't be found, delete it or make the claim more conservative. Never invent data. Label simplified teaching models as "illustrative". Get the maths and simulations right.
12. **Teach only what is needed, but teach it fully; history and philosophy woven into every chapter, never separate chapters**:
    - **Deletion test**: if this sentence went, would the reader understand the core concept less well? If not, delete it. Keep timelines to 3–5 points by default (only the points that move the story; more are not remembered). Give one or two representative names, versions, products or parameters. Take implementation details only as far as "why it matters". Keep the body at moderate difficulty and stop once the reader "gets it"; derivations, maths and research details go into drawers collapsed by default, leaving depth for readers who want it. Cut irrelevant detail, never explanation. Every new concept, example and story is explained until a reader with no background truly understands. Two or three extra sentences that fully explain one point beat one sentence each on five points. The explanation of a core concept stays in the prose: the concept card is only a summary, and the prose says what question each core concept answers, where its boundary is, and gives one example; anything the reader needs to understand it never goes into a collapsed drawer, which holds only derivations and extensions.
    - **History** is the invention story (item 9), making clear why it had to exist.
    - **Philosophy moments**: aim for one per chapter, provided the chapter's concepts really raise a question worth thinking about (2–3 positions with who holds each, no model answer, an open question at the end). Aim for at least one per part; when a chapter, or a whole part, has no fitting question, skip it rather than pad.

## 1A. Chapter acceptance checklist

Three tiers. **Principles** (required) decide whether the reader can follow; every chapter is checked, and how to meet them is flexible. **Optional practices** are used when the content suits them, and held to the standard when used. **Technical floor** is checked in every chapter and does not move. The numbers in the tables are defaults, each with what it protects; change them if the course does not need them, and record the change in the project document.

**Difficulty points** (the outline scores each concept once, relative to the course's reader): a base of 1 = names or groups something the reader already has experience of; 2 = asks the reader to change an old idea (a new distinction, a counter-intuitive conclusion); 3 = asks the reader to build a new moving model (a relation between quantities, a causal chain, an iteration). +1 for a new symbol or formula; +1 for depending on a concept just taught in the chapter that is not yet solid. A prerequisite taught in an earlier chapter scores 0, with one sentence back-reference, plus a reminder figure or table where readers easily forget; if it was not taught, it is a new concept of this chapter and scores in full. The method is deliberately rough and is a planning estimate, not a hard cap: its purpose is to let people see before drafting that "this chapter won't fit", and to decide where to split or give more room, never to pad or split mechanically to hit a number.

Calibration: a chapter on "what is a game" with four everyday concepts (the four elements 1, kinds of play 1, "family resemblance" 2, "the playful attitude" 2, plus a small exercise) comes to about 7 points, within budget; a chapter that teaches gradients, slope, learning rate and local minima together comes to about 23 points and should be split into about three chapters.

| Length rule | Default (what it protects) |
|---|---|
| Chapter total | ≤ 8 points, about 40 minutes, as a warning line (the reader finishes in one sitting without overload); short introductory courses can use ≤ 6; adjust after reader review |
| Heavy concepts | At most 2 concepts of ≥ 3 points per chapter (each heavy concept needs its own section, with a hand calculation or interaction) |
| Room per point | 1 point: 1–2 paragraphs + 1 visual, and several 1-point concepts can share a section; 2 points: its own section with one first-hand experience for the reader; 3 points: its own section with a hand calculation or interaction; ≥ 4 points: at least two sections, or a chapter of its own |
| Over budget | Split at the course-plan stage, not by cramming while drafting; existing courses are not split retroactively, decide when rewriting |

|---|---|---|

## 1C. Writing style

Body prose style (sentences, paragraphs, banned words, headings, dialogue, emphasis marks, openings and structure) follows the `en-teaching-prose` skill entirely. Briefs for writing and review agents include its path and tell them to read SKILL.md and both references first. Each course's project document adds only the conventions specific to that course.

The writing requirements below sit outside the skill and come from the course structure:

- **No meta-language**: the body never says "this chapter", "this course", "the next section covers" or "chapter N explains this in detail". Natural back-references are fine ("remember the hide-and-seek example?"), and links between chapters are left to "related chapters".
- **Open with cause and context, then bring in the concept**: a chapter must open with cause and context, usually in one of four ways (everyday scene, same-structure analogy, lay out the task, a question; see `en-teaching-prose`; another way is fine if the reader can say after the opening what is being done and why); a reference to an earlier chapter takes one sentence, and the data, definitions and worked numbers a chapter uses are shown in that chapter, so it can be read without the last one. When the main concept gets its name is covered in item 2 of section 1.
- **Concepts take the stage, the story stays behind** (courses with a background story or characters): the story appears only in the short opening briefing, the header picture and an occasional line from the companion, and a background story or level briefing never replaces the opening screen's cause and context (it goes in the header area or a collapsed box after the opening). The prose is about the concept itself, with examples from real situations in the field, and interactive tasks use the concept's own terms ("get the loss below 0.5 within 6 steps"). Never replace a concept's name with a story stand-in, such as calling loss "enemy HP"; readers could not recognise it in the real world afterwards. A briefing must be self-consistent: one causal chain that holds together (what the opponent or object is → what is wrong → what to do), using only words that have already been named.
  ✗ A briefing says "entropy is eating away at the camp; steady it", yet entropy has not been taught, so the reader knows neither what the opponent is nor what to do.
- **Concepts lead, maths supports** (courses with maths; the user's words: "no big blocks of isolated maths", "I don't want it to feel like a stiff textbook"):
  - Every formula belongs to a concept and appears right next to that concept's diagram or interaction. The reader understands the concept first, and the formula then arrives as shorthand.
  - No symbol tables in the body, no pure-maths sections or "supply depots" crammed with several concepts (in maths-heavy courses a symbol cheat-sheet may sit in a collapsed drawer). Explain each symbol in passing, inside the sentence where it first appears.
  - Derivations and extra maths go into an "advanced drawer" at the end of the chapter, collapsed by default.
  - Important formulas form "families" that recur across chapters (the same pattern met again in different topics), which helps readers build meaning.
- **Maths climbs five steps**:
  1. Present the situation and the trouble first, so the reader needs a method;
  2. Work the example through with concrete numbers, no letters;
  3. Point out the pattern and swap words for symbols one at a time, ≤2 new symbols each time, saying on the spot how each is read and which number it stands for;
  4. Turn key formulas into a "formula breakdown" interaction: formula fragments and plain-language explanations linked by matching colours;
  5. Work it again with the formula and a live diagram (move a slider, the diagram changes at once).
  The five steps are the full climb for a hard formula; for a simple expression the reader sees at a glance, merge steps 2 and 3 and skip steps 4 and 5. Before any mathematical concept or symbol appears, prepare the ground with at least one of a real-world situation, a visualisation or a numerical example, and give more the harder and more abstract it is. Settle the reader's mathematical starting point first, and treat anything beyond it as new knowledge to prepare on the spot. The goal is **mathematical intuition**: what it resembles, why it has this shape, and what happens when one number changes. Better one formula fewer than one formula the reader can't follow.
- **Make readers think, not feel they are reading a textbook**: each section opens so that the reader feels a question waiting for an answer (it need not be phrased as a question), lets the reader guess and try first at the key points and discover the pattern in an interaction, and only then makes it explicit and names it. When more sections follow, a section ends by leaving the question the reader themselves now wants to ask, not a summary.
- **Self-check**: read it through with the headings covered. Does it flow, and is it clear why each sentence is there? If it reads like an outline, rewrite it (the question chain is in section 1, item 1; how to write headings is in `en-teaching-prose`, under chapter titles and headings).

## 1D. Worldview knowledge base and weaving rules

This kind of knowledge lets readers "see the world differently", and the user especially likes it. It is woven into each chapter's "why it works" and **spirals back** in 2–3 situations. A principle with enough weight can become its own "worldview chapter" (a law the reader can verify by hand, not a philosophical ramble), set up earlier by letting the reader meet it in concrete phenomena (without naming later chapters) and referred back to later. Verify sources online before use.

**Weaving rules**:
1. Build a "principle–chapter" matrix: each important principle by default appears in ≥2 chapters in different situations (the spiral is what gives it weight), and each chapter carries 0–2 principles: only when the chapter's concepts really have a principle to hang on it, never to fill the matrix.
2. A principle appears under a concrete phenomenon ("Why are gacha pulls so compelling?" → prediction error).
3. On its second appearance, refer back to the first and take a new angle.
4. Cite sources and controversies; when there is a relationship map, register the principle as a node in it, linked to the concepts that use it.
5. Worldview chapters also follow the five-layer structure; when the law can be checked first-hand, add an interaction that verifies it.

## 2. Interaction and visualisation

- **Signature interactions** are direct manipulation like "taking a house apart": readers take apart, assemble, drag, tune parameters and build with their own hands, and see the concept's consequences immediately. Controls are few and clear, and a "Try it" task gives the purpose. At key points, "predict first, then see the result". Multiple choice belongs only in practice questions (prediction questions that reveal the answer afterwards are the exception).
- **Feedback**: every click produces a visible change, and everything can be undone or reset.
- **Diagram types**: relationship, flow, cycle, coordinate, comparison, timeline, distribution. Text large enough to read on a phone.
- **Real images**: real photos of historical figures, objects, places, paper pages and so on help reading and keep interest. Use only public-domain or CC-licensed images (prefer Wikimedia Commons, NASA, government works), download them into the course, write source and licence in the caption, and register them in the course's image credits list. If the licence can't be found, don't use it.
- **Picture layout**: when long text has an illustration, set them side by side, one column of text and one of picture on wide screens, aligned at the top, with no text wrap and no vertical centring; stack them on narrow screens. Group examples in tabs or a carousel showing one at a time, with counterexamples clearly marked.
- **Illustrations draw the "why"**: a positive example shows every element present; a counterexample shows the missing element or what went wrong. Each course builds its own illustration library (characters, objects, drawing tools), and chapters draw on top of it.
- **Rules for illustrated characters**:
  - Characters are drawn the same way across the course; different characters are told apart by hairstyle, hats, glasses, clothing and accessories, not colour alone.
  - Physically plausible: standing people have feet on the ground, seated people have a chair or stool; a hand holding something really grips it (the object sits between both hands, not floating off them or pressed against the body), and a pointing finger points at something in the picture; people and robots have all their hands and feet; eyes are visible behind glasses.
  - Text doesn't cover pictures: speech bubbles and labels don't overlap characters or shapes; bubble text is short; characters aren't drawn too small.
  - Screenshot each illustration on its own and inspect it enlarged (`tools/shot.mjs` captures a single element).
## 3. Structure and navigation

- **In-chapter navigation** (generated by shared scripts): collapsible sections, chapter contents (highlighting the current section, expand/collapse all, back to top), reading progress, everything expanded when printing.
- **Learning-aid components**: concept cards, principle boxes, invention-story boxes, stance cards and the like each have a component (`components.md`), plus progress ticks (headings carry no layer labels; see section 1, item 9).
- **Small variations between chapters**: chapters in the same part vary slightly in main colour, background texture and title decoration, while the style's skeleton (fonts, component shapes, stroke style) stays the same.

**Optional features table**: when starting a new course, consider each one, decide, and record the decision in the project document. Courses that readers look things up in and practise with repeatedly benefit most.

| Feature | What it does for readers | When it's worth adding | How |
|---|---|---|---|
| Learning map | See at a glance how the course divides and what to learn first | Many chapters, with prerequisites | Map on the contents page grouped by part |
| Learning paths | Different readers take different routes | Readers start from very different places | A few routes on the contents page, such as "one-week intro", "full route", "underlying principles route" |
| Concept relationship map | See prerequisite, explanation, application and contrast relations between concepts | Many interlinked concepts | `map.html` + `js/concepts.js` (nodes = concepts, edges = relations; code in `kit/modules/graph.js`) |
| Related chapters | Know which chapters to read next | Generated alongside a relationship map | `kit/modules/vl-concepts.js` generates it from `js/concepts.js` |
| Hover definitions | Look up terms without leaving the text | Many terms, readers with no background | Shared script + term data |
| Glossary | Look up all terms in one place | Many terms | `glossary.html`, data from the `terms` in `js/concepts.js` (collected from the chapters by `kit/modules/build-concepts.mjs --write`) |
| Flashcards | Memorise terms and key points | Lots to memorise | `flashcards.html` |
| Review chapter | Tie everything together at the end | Long courses | Final chapter |
| Notes / "Your thoughts" | Readers write down their own ideas | Open questions, philosophy moments | Auto-saving text box |
| Sound | Feedback or atmosphere | Gamified courses, young readers | Muted by default, can be turned off |

## 4. Chapter structure: outline first, fixed shell

Before drafting, write a one-page outline for each chapter and keep it in the course folder as `章纲/chNN.md`. An outline is far cheaper than a chapter, so problems of order, prerequisites and jumps are cheapest to fix here. The outline is a planning and review tool, not a form to fill mechanically; other formats such as game levels write their own structure in the project document, but the principles of section 1 and the five layers must all be there.

**Outline template**

1. Driving question: one line in the reader's words; a guided tour around one theme can be broad.
2. Reader's starting point: concepts this chapter can use directly (taught in earlier chapters), and the reader's maths and background.
3. How it opens: everyday scene / same-structure analogy / lay out the task / a question (see `en-teaching-prose`), one line on the concrete content.
4. Question chain:

| Section | Question now (only words already known) | Answer of this section (one line) | New things (by default ≤ 1 per paragraph) | How the reader meets it first-hand | Question left |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|

5. Concepts and difficulty: one row per concept: name, base points, +symbol, +dependency, total, depends on, section where it is made solid, the counterexample that marks its edge, the moment the reader uses it.
6. Element table: one row each: element (component number), which question or which reading experience it serves, what the reader does or looks at, what they understand or feel afterwards.
7. Key examples: one main example, plus one line each for the counterexample and the rest.
8. Collapsed material: one line each for the invention story, derivations and side paths.

**The chapter shell** (fixed order; the sections in the middle follow the question chain): header (chapter-number badge + a title that says the topic outright + one-line subtitle + small illustration; wording of title and subtitle in `en-teaching-prose`) → learning goals (3 items of "after reading you can…" + reading time) → opening → sections following the question chain → common misconceptions (written only when there are cross-concept misunderstandings, and only those) → key points + one-sentence summary → practice (the last chapter of each part adds "part review + synthesis questions") → "Your thoughts" text box (optional, auto-saving) → previous/next chapter, with related chapters when a relationship map exists (generated by shared script). Philosophy moments, invention-story boxes and principle boxes are optional pieces placed in the middle sections wherever the content wants them, with no fixed position.

## 5. Files and numbering

Below is the default structure, adjusted per course (a game course might use `level-xxx.html`, for example). Files for optional features exist only if those features were chosen.

## 6. Chinese–English bilingual

- English lives in same-named files under `en/`; the top bar and contents page have a language switch; progress and notes share storage keys across both languages.
- Fully translate body text, in-picture text, interaction JS strings, quizzes, placeholders and term definitions; ids, structure and logic stay unchanged; English also follows 1C; if `js/concepts.js` exists, it includes English fields too.
- Translate only after the Chinese is final; show the language switch only once the English is complete.

## 7. Technical constraints

- Pure front end, works offline, opens with a double-click on the html file; storage uses the shared script's wrapper with try/catch.
- **Do it natively when possible.** When a library is really needed: ① download it into the course's `lib/` at a fixed version, with no CDN dependency; ② load it only on pages that need it; ③ choose a permissive licence (MIT and similar) and record name, purpose, version and licence in `lib/README.md`; ④ no frameworks that need a build step. Libraries worth evaluating: Rough.js (hand-drawn), KaTeX (formulas), p5.js (generative art), Matter.js (physics), D3 (data visualisation), Three.js (3D), Tone.js (music).
- Web fonts are inserted as `<link>` asynchronously by JS, with system-font fallbacks; never `@import` web fonts in CSS (on a slow network it blocks the whole page's scripts).
- After `</main>`, load the kit scripts before the page scripts; page-specific styles go in an in-page `<style>`, with ids and class names prefixed by chapter, and never redefine shared class names (that overrides the shared styles).
- Canvas uses pointer events; single column at phone width, with large enough touch targets; print styles included.
- **Windows environment**: temporary files use absolute scratchpad paths (`/tmp` in Git Bash and in Python are not the same place); Python scripts that print Chinese need `PYTHONIOENCODING=utf-8` (the console is GBK); edit large chunks of Chinese with the Edit tool, not a shell heredoc.

## 8. Workflow and quality gates

**Pipeline for each chapter** (items 3–6 give the details of each step):

| Step | What happens | Who | To pass |
|---|---|---|---|
| ① Outline | Write `章纲/chNN.md` from the section 4 template | Writing agent | Difficulty total within the warning line (or with a reason); every row of the element table can say what it serves |
| ② Logic review | Reads only the outline and the project document's reader profile; checks order, prerequisites, jumps and load | Agent with no context (brief in item 5) | None of: "a question uses a new word not yet taught", "the answer doesn't lead to the next question", "a dependency was not made solid first" |
| ③ User approves the outline | Sample chapter and the first chapter of each part one by one; the rest are merged into one document per part for a skim | User | User agrees; the user's changes are made in the outline |
| ④ Draft | Write to the outline; change the outline first if it must change; when restructuring a whole chapter, back up the old page first and rebuild the structure from the new outline instead of layering patches on the old page | Writing agent | For a heavy chapter (total near the warning line or with a concept of ≥ 4 points), run a quick reader review after the first two sections (part A only) before writing on |
| ⑤ Script checks | `check.mjs --fast` down to 0 → full run down to 0 → look at screenshots | Writing agent | No ✗; each hint line (2e style included) is answered with "fixed" or "kept on purpose, because…"; the prose has been self-reviewed sentence by sentence against the style skill's review.md |
| ⑥ Reader review | Reads the finished chapter, logs section by section, rebuilds the question chain, tests itself closed-book | Agent with no context (brief in item 5) | The lead compares with the outline and sends the list of changes back to the writing agent |
| ⑦ Lead acceptance | Runs the full check, looks at screenshots, scans all headings, reads section by section against the outline | Lead | Subagent reports can be wrong too; screenshots and check results are not enough, so read at least the opening section and one middle section of prose |
| ⑧ User sees the result | Sample chapter one by one; afterwards one chapter per part | User | — |

1. Ask for the visual style first (section 10), then readers, length, language and modules, then give the user a course plan (knowledge map, chapter table with each chapter's driving question and difficulty total, what readers can do afterwards) to approve; how to ask: course-kit `SKILL.md` section 6. Present a plan first for big changes such as renumbering or adding chapters.
2. Settle the structure first (sections 4 and 5 by default, or the course's own) and the optional features → build the shared skeleton (generated with `starter/new-course.mjs`: styles, kit, contents page, chapter table, illustration library, chosen optional features) → make one sample chapter for the user to approve the outline, writing and layout → then roll out. Before writing chapters in parallel, the sample chapter's prose style passes the user's review first: once a style fault sets in, it shows up in a dozen chapters at once.
3. Briefs for subagents are complete and self-contained: chapter table, the chapter outline, required reading, prose style (include the `en-teaching-prose` path, or `zh-teaching-prose` for Chinese, and paste the course's most important style rules with their counterexamples verbatim instead of compressing them to "follow the skill"), assigned worldview principles, fact-checking requirements, interaction list, checking requirements and report format. Subagents change only their own files and never touch shared files; the same batch of files is never given to two agents at once; temporary files go in each agent's own subfolder of the scratchpad. When a subagent is interrupted (for example by running out of quota), continue it by message instead of starting over. How many run in parallel is set by each course's project document.
4. **Three checks per chapter** (one command, `node <skill>/tools/check.mjs <absolute course folder path> chNN`, runs them all; steps and flow in course-kit `SKILL.md` section 7):
   - **Syntax**: extract the inline `<script>` blocks and run each through `new Function(code)`.
   - **Runtime**: `node tools/qa.mjs <absolute course folder path> [chNN]` clicks every button and drags every slider at 420px and 1100px, checking for JS errors and horizontal overflow. Always pass an absolute path (a relative path is rejected); Chinese pages and English pages under `en/` are both tested.
   - **Visual**: `node tools/shot.mjs <absolute html path> <output.png> page <width>` captures the full page (one at 420 and one at 1100); then capture single illustrations or charts and inspect them enlarged: does the picture show, does text overlap, is contrast enough. Don't use `msedge --screenshot` directly (layout is wrong below about 500px).
   - The report pastes every hint line (2c experience before names, 2d load, 2e style, wiring hints) and answers each with "fixed" or "kept on purpose, because…"; the lead spot-checks.
5. **Two kinds of review**, both by agents with no context; the brief states identity, input and a fixed report format:
   - **Logic review (reads the outline)**: the identity is a teaching-design editor who does not touch the prose and checks only the logic. Input: `章纲/chNN.md`, the project document's reader profile (reader, maths starting point), and the concept list of earlier chapters. For each finding give "place | problem | suggestion": ① does each section's "question now" use only words from the reader's starting point or words already named in this chapter; ② does each answer naturally raise the next section's question, or is there a hard cut; ③ is any paragraph's load of new things too high; ④ was a depended-on concept made solid first; ⑤ were difficulty points scored too low (compare with the 1A calibration) and does the total cross the warning line; ⑥ does the element table have a row that serves neither understanding nor the reading experience; ⑦ does each core concept have an edge-marking counterexample and a moment where the reader uses it; ⑧ does the opening let the reader say what is being done and why. The last line answers: should this chapter be split, and how.
   - **Reader review (reads the finished chapter)**: the identity is the course's target reader exactly as the project document describes it, with the maths and background starting point stated; it sees neither the outline nor any earlier discussion. Input: the output of `PYTHONIOENCODING=utf-8 python <skill>/tools/readable.py <absolute html path>`, which marks the right answers (correct options in quizzes and guess-then-reveal carry ✓, estimates state the answer, ordering tasks list the correct order), so the brief must say: read the prose only, do the exercises and note your own answers, then compare with the ✓ marks. The report has five parts. **A, section log** (one line after each section, never revised: "section N | what I understood (one line, own words) | what I now want to ask | which word, number or sentence lost me (first 15 characters)"); **B, rebuilt from memory** (with the text closed: what question the chapter answers; how each section follows the last; each core concept in own words plus an example not in the text); **C, closed-book exercises** (for each wrong answer, "why I chose that"); **D, elements** (for each interaction and figure: "what it made me understand" or "what it gave me as a reader", and "useless" if neither; any word in an interaction task I did not yet know; scripted replays cover every task's finish condition and numbers; counter-example: one interaction that crams fog, a trail, a health bar, a curve and two tasks into one board); **E, pacing and voice** (where is there a wall of text, where must I remember too much at once; AI voice per `en-teaching-prose/references/review.md`, only the five clearest). No pleasantries, no overall verdict.
   - How the lead uses it: compare part B's question chain with the outline row by row, and the section where they differ is the break; where part A's "what I want to ask" differs from the next section's question, the link is broken; wrong answers in part C go back to the outline to see whether the concept was made solid; elements marked "useless" in part D are cut first; for concepts where sticking points cluster, raise the difficulty score by one.
6. **Lead acceptance**: after a subagent delivers, the lead takes screenshots and looks for themselves, scans all headings separately, reads the finished chapter against the outline to see whether it teaches what the outline said, and rechecks the subagent's "unverified/uncertain" list. Subagent reports can be wrong too.
7. When everything is done, run a health check (by hand) against 1A and 1B item by item, focusing on whether the question chain holds together, whether invention stories make clear "why it had to exist", whether interactions are useful rather than piled up, whether the language is natural, and whether there are any forward previews.
8. Then build the chosen optional features (glossary, flashcards, learning paths and so on), and do the English edition last.
9. On delivery, say honestly what was verified, what wasn't, and which facts are uncertain.

| Problem | Who | How |
|---|---|---|

| Concept highlighting | Script (error) | check.mjs 2b |
| Experience before names, load | Script hint + review | check.mjs 2c, 2d |
| Walls of text | Script (error) + eye | check.mjs 3; screenshots |

| Did the reader really understand, where it broke, which element is useless, AI voice | Reader review | Section log, rebuilt chain, closed-book test |

## 9. Communication preferences

- Reply in Chinese, answer the question first and then give the next step; be concise, preferring tables and lists; give a clear recommendation instead of listing options.
- Report honestly without exaggerating; "syntax passes" is not "tested".
- Look at the target before irreversible actions such as renaming, deleting or overwriting; don't keep asking for approval on a plan the user has already confirmed.
- After feedback, fix one sample chapter and show the user before rolling it out. When the user says "I don't get it", ask where they got stuck before rewriting.
- **Edit documents in place**: find the closest existing entry and rewrite it, or delete the conflicting old entry; never append "supplement/new" sections, and keep section numbers and titles unchanged; write a rule as "when …" rather than "≥ N per chapter", write any number that must stay as "default + why", and keep the three tiers (principle, optional practice, technical floor) distinct; on conflict, the user's latest words win; use the same word for the same thing throughout a document, prefer tables and short lists, and start each document with one line stating its purpose. This applies only to documents; course pages, by contrast, should vary their structure and elements from chapter to chapter.

## 10. Visual style and shared aesthetics

**Choosing a style**: the first thing when a course starts is to let the user pick one of the approved skins in course-kit's `skins/` (one line each on what it suits, with its approval page; you may say which you recommend and why, but never pick for them), and start only after the user chooses. The chosen skin decides the look; content structure, interactions, navigation and writing rules stay the same.

**Shared aesthetic principles (all styles)**:
- **Readability first**: body text, secondary text, captions and in-picture text all have ≥4.5:1 contrast against their background, with no small light-grey text; long text sits on a quiet background.
- **Fonts**: calligraphy, chalk and similar decorative fonts only for large titles; handwriting fonts verified as legible (with a clear font for letters and digits) may be used for subheadings, buttons, labels and in-picture text; body text uses a clear font. Style comes through colour, borders, texture, illustration and numeral fonts.
- **No cheap slide-deck cards**: long text sits directly on the ground (wall, paper, page), never on a card floating over a flat colour. Only "things" get physical form (frame and mat, mounted board, display case, with shadows falling the same way). The ground has a subtle real texture, and light has a source.
- **Hierarchy and white space**: titles, body and notes are clearly layered, with breathing room, and decoration never competes with the body.
- **Pictures serve understanding**: diagrams and effects must help understanding or give feedback on actions; motion can be turned off, respecting the "reduce motion" preference.
- **Style = one stylesheet + a little decoration** (colour variables, font stack, borders, texture, illustration strokes, badges); navigation, quizzes, carousels, relationship maps and other scripts are style-independent and reused as they are.
- **Verify filters**: when using SVG filters for hand-drawn jitter and similar effects, write the attribute as `in="SourceGraphic"` (one wrong word turns the whole picture to noise), and keep text outside the filter group.
