# Worked examples

Most rules in SKILL.md come from cases like these. Learn the reasoning behind each fix rather than copying the sentences. Read them by tier: a dropped-in opening, a hard cut or a wrong connective breaks a principle and must be fixed, while antitheses, labelling, formulaic openers and em dashes are AI-voice signals, to be fixed when they come in a run and judged in context when alone. The paragraph rewrite in section 2 tells a history, so it follows the timeline; explaining a concept does not need to. The material comes from two of the user's courses: an AI course chapter on what AI is (the Chinese Room, the AI effect, narrow and general AI) and a game design chapter on what a game is (Suits, Caillois).

## 2. A paragraph diagnosis and rewrite

**Original** (AI course, the section after the "spectrum" of machines):

> The goalposts of intelligence keep moving — and that's a crucial insight. Chess was once seen as the pinnacle of human intellect. Then, in 1997, IBM's Deep Blue defeated world champion Garry Kasparov, and suddenly it was "just brute-force calculation." Reading, dictation, navigation: each was an AI problem before it was solved, and just a phone feature after.
>
> This phenomenon has a name: the AI effect. In her book on the early history of AI, *Machines Who Think*, the science writer Pamela McCorduck noted that whenever a computer did something new, someone would say "that's not thinking."
>
> Deep Blue also illustrates something else. It could beat the world champion, but it couldn't fold a shirt. AI that's good at only one thing is called narrow AI, and almost all AI today is narrow.
>
> A five-year-old can't beat anyone at chess, but she can learn a new game in ten minutes. AI that can learn anything new like a human is called general AI. Whether and when it will arrive is hotly debated.
>
> Ultimately, from calculators to chatbots, every machine on the spectrum runs on rules. Where those rules come from is the pivotal question of seventy years of AI.

**Why it sounds like AI:**

1. **A template of antitheses.** "An AI problem before / a phone feature after", "beat the world champion / couldn't fold a shirt" and "can't beat anyone / learns in ten minutes" come in a row, all with the same build-up and knock-down.
2. **Labelling instead of telling.** Every paragraph runs example, then definition: Deep Blue gives the AI effect, the shirt gives narrow AI, the child gives general AI. It reads like a checklist being ticked.
3. **Hard cuts.** "Deep Blue also illustrates something else" and the sudden five-year-old arrive with no link to what came before.
4. **Stock AI tells.** The em dash plus "crucial insight", "pinnacle", "This phenomenon has a name:", "Ultimately," and "the pivotal question", and the paragraph ends on an exam-style summary.
5. **Clunky premodification.** "In her book on the early history of AI, *Machines Who Think*, the science writer…" piles up material before the subject arrives. Split it, or put the subject first.

**Rewrite:**

> On this spectrum, the mark that says "intelligent" has never stayed put. For a long time chess counted as a peak of human thought. In 1997 IBM's Deep Blue beat the world champion Garry Kasparov, and the reaction soon turned from awe to fault-finding: it was only calculating fast. The same thing kept happening. Reading handwriting, taking dictation and finding a route were all hard AI problems until machines could do them, and then they became ordinary features on a phone. The science writer Pamela McCorduck had noticed this "AI effect" back in 1979, in *Machines Who Think*: each time a computer managed something new, someone said that wasn't really thinking.
>
> The fault-finders had a point. Deep Blue could beat Kasparov across sixty-four squares, but away from the board it could not recognise a shirt, let alone fold one. Nearly every AI system in use today, the chatbots included, is narrow AI of this kind, strong on one track and lost off it.
>
> A five-year-old shows the other possibility. She loses every game of chess she plays, yet teach her a new game and she has the hang of it in ten minutes. Researchers call that ability to cope with unfamiliar situations general intelligence, and building it in a machine is the long-standing goal. Whether machines will get there, and when, is still argued over.
>
> However different the machines on the spectrum look, from the simplest calculator to a chatbot that answers fluently, each one does the same thing underneath: in situation A, follow rule A. A calculator follows the rules of arithmetic, and a ghost in Pac-Man chases the player with a few lines of code. Further along the spectrum, the situations become harder to predict and the number of rules multiplies. So where were all those rules supposed to come from?

**What the rewrite does:**

1. **Erases the glossary entries.** "The AI effect", "narrow AI" and "general intelligence" each appear inside a sentence that is telling the story.
2. **A concrete scene replaces the abstract link.** "The fault-finders had a point" picks up the reaction at the end of the first paragraph and brings the eye back to the chessboard.
3. **Shared attention.** "A five-year-old shows the other possibility" turns the reader's head from Deep Blue to the child.
4. **The tension builds to a question.** The last paragraph lays out what calculators, ghosts and chatbots have in common, lets the rules multiply, and ends on the question the next section answers.

Note: the "AI effect" phrasing is commonly traced to McCorduck. Her best-known statement of it is in later editions of *Machines Who Think* (first published 1979, 2nd edition 2004). Cite the book without quoting exact wording unless it has been checked against the text.
